# Knowledge-Base Governance

Reduce conflicting copies in a shared knowledge base by making authority, navigation, and change verification explicit.

## Workflow

1. **Inventory the material.** Documents, owners, audiences, update frequency, links, and each item's type: policy, procedure, evidence file, template, or navigation pointer.
2. **Assign authority.** For every rule or fact, designate one authoritative location. Mark supporting copies as summaries or pointers and link them back. Never declare a document authoritative just because it is newest or easiest to find.
3. **Organize around user tasks.** Group content in the order readers actually do the work. Keep templates, source records, procedures, and navigation aids where the platform and the organization's policy say they belong.
4. **Choose an edit method.** For a shared or actively edited document, inspect revision and collaboration state first, then prefer a narrow targeted edit. Use full replacement only when the document is controlled and a recoverable prior version exists.
5. **Preserve change traceability.** Record what changed, why, who approved the rule when approval is required, and which linked copies or indexes need updating.
6. **Read back after edits.** Confirm the saved revision advanced and the intended content is actually present. Open important links and compare derived copies against the authoritative source.
7. **Run a closeout scan.** Search for stale terminology, broken links, duplicate rules, and pointers to missing resources. Report unresolved ownership or approval questions without inventing answers.

## Governance principles

- One rule, one designated source of authority; every copy points back to it.
- A navigation pointer exists to help the reader find the answer, not to become a second maintained copy of it.
- Keep templates and records distinct from procedural guidance when that separation protects a single authoritative copy.
- Revision metadata and content readback are separate checks. A newer revision does not prove the right content was saved.
- Link and permission checks must reflect the intended audience. Access by the editor alone proves nothing.
- Preserve an audit trail for consequential changes; never overwrite shared work without checking collaboration state.

## Pitfalls

- **Copy-paste converts a pointer into a second copy.** Once it is a copy, it will drift.
- **Concurrent editing**: a revision number moving forward can be someone else's save, not yours. Read back the specific content you changed.
- **Link permission is per reader.** The editor being able to open a link does not mean the target audience can.
- **"Latest file wins" is not authority.** The newest copy is often the least reviewed one.
- **Deleted or moved pages** leave pointers that resolve to nothing; a closeout scan must actually open them.

## Acceptance checks

- Every rule has exactly one named authoritative location, and all copies are marked as pointers.
- Readback evidence exists for each edited document: the specific content, not just a revision bump.
- Links were opened as an intended reader would, and the result recorded.
- The closeout scan lists stale terms, duplicate rules, and broken pointers that were removed.
- Open ownership or approval questions are listed as open, not silently resolved.
